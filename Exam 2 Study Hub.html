Ch 18 — Carboxylic Acid Derivatives (Nucleophilic Acyl Substitution)

🧭 START HERE — the one idea that makes this whole chapter easy.

There is really only ONE reaction in this chapter: a nucleophile kicks out a leaving group on a C=O carbon. Everything else is just bookkeeping on top of that:

  1. WHICH derivative am I starting from? That sets how reactive it is and what the leaving group is (the Reactivity Ladder below).
  2. WHAT is the nucleophile? Water, alcohol, amine, hydride, carbon (Grignard/cuprate)… that decides the product.
  3. HOW does the mechanism run? Always the same 5-step skeleton — PT-NA-PT-LLG-PT — and the number of steps depends only on whether the nucleophile is acid-catalyzed (5), neutral (3), or negative (2). → full rule in the Mechanisms tab

Rule of the ladder: you can only convert a more reactive derivative into a less reactive one (down the ladder). To climb back up you must hydrolyze to the carboxylic acid, then re-activate with SOCl₂. Memorize the ladder picture and the reagent-decision guide just below, and 80% of Ch 18 questions answer themselves.

The Reactivity Ladder — memorize this picture

Acid chloride RCOCl — most reactive (LG = Cl⁻)
Anhydride RCO–O–OCR (LG = carboxylate) — same reactions as RCOCl
Ester RCO–OR′ (LG = R′O⁻)
Amide RCO–NR₂ (LG = ⁻NR₂) — least reactive C=O
Nitrile R–C≡N (no C=O; its own chemistry)

Rule: you can only convert down the ladder (reactive → less reactive). Amide is a dead end. To go UP: hydrolyze back to RCOOH (H₂O, + H⁺/heat for stubborn ones), then SOCl₂.

Why: Cl pulls electrons (no resonance donation) → very electrophilic C=O. N donates strongly by resonance → stabilized, unreactive C=O.

Trap 1: LAH on an amide gives an amine (C=O → CH₂), not an alcohol — the odd one out. Aldol reactions MECHANISM TESTED (2025 C)

2 RCH₂CHO — NaOH, H₂O → β-hydroxy aldehyde (aldol addition) — heat → α,β-unsaturated aldehyde + H₂O (aldol condensation)

Product pattern: α-carbon of one partner bonds to carbonyl-C of the other. New OH is β to the C=O. Heat = lose H₂O between α and β.

Crossed aldol: only clean if one partner has no α-H (e.g., benzaldehyde, formaldehyde) or if you use LDA first to pick the nucleophile ("directed aldol": 1) LDA −78° on ketone, 2) add the aldehyde, 3) H₂O).

Nitro-aldol (textbook): nitroalkane α-H (pKa ≈ 10!) + aldehyde, base → β-nitro alcohol; then H₂/Ni reduces NO₂ → NH₂ giving a β-aminoalcohol. Recognize "OH and NH₂ on adjacent carbons" → nitro-aldol then reduce.

Intramolecular aldol: 1,4- or 1,5-diketone + NaOH/heat → 5- or 6-membered enone ring (never 3/4-membered). Prof's 2023 rules: cyclizations ALWAYS go to the condensation (enone, never stop at the alcohol); if the molecule has a ketone AND an aldehyde, the ketone forms the enolate and attacks the aldehyde (aldehyde = better electrophile); if two different α-carbons each give a 5- or 6-ring, draw both products.

When is it condensation vs addition? No heat → addition (β-OH). Heat → condensation (enone). Conjugation possible in the product (e.g., aryl partner) → ALWAYS condensation regardless of temperature. Cyclization → always condensation.

Retro-aldol: heat + base runs it backwards — recognize β-hydroxy carbonyls.

Aldol in pictures: 2 acetaldehyde → β-hydroxy aldehyde (addition, cold) → heat → conjugated enone (condensation). The new C–C bond is always α-of-one to carbonyl-C-of-the-other.

Claisen condensation MECHANISM TESTED (2025 A & B)

2 CH₃CO–OEt — 1) NaOEt 2) H₃O⁺ → β-ketoester (acetoacetic ester)

Like aldol but the ester has a leaving group (OEt) → C=O reforms and kicks out OEt instead of staying tetrahedral. Final deprotonation of the doubly-α H by OEt⁻ drives the equilibrium; H₃O⁺ workup returns it.

Dieckmann: intramolecular Claisen — a 1,6- or 1,7-diester → 5- or 6-membered ring β-ketoester.

Claisen prerequisites (2023 lecture): the α-carbon needs ≥ 2 H's (the final deprotonation between the two C=O's is what drives the equilibrium forward — no 2nd α-H, no Claisen). And NEVER use NaOH on an ester — it saponifies instead.

Crossed Claisen: clean when one ester has no α-H (e.g., ethyl benzoate + ethyl acetate → benzoylacetate). The textbook's full no-α-H acceptor list (use it in excess): ethyl formate (HCO₂Et → installs CHO), diethyl carbonate (→ installs CO₂Et, makes a malonate-type diester), diethyl oxalate, ethyl benzoate.

Claisen in pictures: 2 ethyl acetate → β-ketoester (ethyl acetoacetate). Looks like aldol, but OEt⁻ got kicked out, so the C=O comes back instead of staying an alcohol.

Baeyer–Villiger oxidation (MCPBA + ketone → ESTER) — 2023 lecture

Ketone + MCPBA → ester (oxygen inserted next to the C=O). Same MCPBA that made epoxides from alkenes in Orgo 1. Aldehyde + MCPBA → carboxylic acid (H migrates fastest).

Where does the O go? Between the C=O and the more substituted neighbor. Migration order: H > 3° > cyclohexyl > 2° > aryl/benzyl > 1° > methyl (methyl essentially never migrates). Cyclic ketone → ring-expanded lactone.

1,2 vs 1,4 — SAME enone, two different answers HIGH YIELD

Grignard = 1,2-addition → allylic 3° alcohol (attacks the C=O directly; ring C=C untouched)
Cuprate = 1,4-addition → β-methyl ketone (CH₃ lands on β-carbon; C=O survives, ring C=C gone)
The #1 Michael rule in the practice keys: simple ketone/aldehyde enolates are NOT efficient Michael donors. Good donors = doubly-stabilized enolates (malonate, acetoacetate, 1,3-diketones, β-ketoesters, nitro-alkanes) or enamines (Stork). If the donor side is a plain ketone or aldehyde → the key always switches to a Stork enamine. Good acceptors = α,β-unsaturated ketones, esters, nitriles, and nitro compounds. Malonate anion also opens epoxides at the less-substituted carbon and acylates with RCOCl/anhydrides (21.84).
Enamines do more than Michael (Ch 19 PPT): ① alkylation — enamine + CH₃X / 1° RX / allylic / benzylic / α-haloketone / α-haloester, then H₃O⁺ → α-alkylated ketone. ② acylation — enamine + RCOCl (or anhydride), then H₃O⁺ → β-DIKETONE. Retro-recognition: α-substituted carbonyl ← enamine alkylation; 1,3-diketone ← enamine acylation; 1,5-dicarbonyl ← enamine Michael (Stork).
Prof's 1,2-vs-1,4 sorting rule (2023 lecture): on an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl, Grignard and organolithium attack the carbonyl (1,2); EVERYTHING else attacks the β-carbon (1,4) — cuprates, CN⁻, RS⁻/SH⁻, amines, enolates, enamines. Neutral nucleophiles lose one H in the product. Stork detail: the enamine adds via its MOST substituted α-carbon (opposite of LDA), and enamine alkylation is the mild alternative to LDA when base-sensitive groups are present.
Robinson counting + retro procedure (2023 lecture): after the Michael addition, counting from the donor's α-carbon to the remote C=O is always 6 atoms → 6-membered ring (if your count isn't 6, you attached the acceptor wrong — the β-carbon bonds to the α-carbon FIRST). Retro-Robinson: ① number the ring with the enone C=O side as C1, C=C at C1–C6 ② break C1–C6 (undo the aldol) — C6 becomes a C=O ③ break C4–C5 (undo the Michael) — the C4 side regains the C=C (that's the enone acceptor), the C5 side is the enolate donor.
Retro-aldol (21.75): β-hydroxy ketone + NaOH/heat can run BACKWARDS — HO⁻ deprotonates the β-OH, alkoxide pushes back to C=O, expelling an enolate. Recognize it when a question says "give the reagents and the two carbonyl products." Same logic exists for reverse-Michael: enone + stabilized carbanion re-form.

Diels–Alder — worth ~45 pts on 2025 Form A HIGH YIELD

Diene (s-cis, electron-rich) + dienophile (electron-poor, EWG) — heat → cyclohexene. One step, concerted, no intermediates. Alkyne dienophile → 1,4-cyclohexadiene. Very high temperature runs it backwards (retro-DA).

Diels–Alder in pictures: butadiene (s-cis!) + acrolein (EWG dienophile) → cyclohexene-carbaldehyde. One concerted step — diene ends bond to the dienophile's two alkene carbons.
Exam patterns seen: DA questions are written with "heat" or "150 °C" over the arrow. The prof also tests intramolecular DA ("1 single reagent" = a triene folding onto itself, Exam 3 2023) and exocyclic dienes (2-vinylidene-cyclohexane types). The dienal + HBr question (2025 p9): conjugated dienal + HBr, heat → 1,4-type (thermodynamic) α-bromo product; cold → 1,2 (kinetic) — Form A said heat, Form B said cold, same skeleton.
How to nail the regio question: draw partial charges. δ− carbon of diene (the one pushed on by OMe) bonds to δ+ β-carbon of the dienophile. Prof's review shows exactly this with MeO-diene + acrolein → "para" major.
Two practice-set extras: ① AlCl₃ (Lewis acid) accelerates Diels–Alder — it coordinates the dienophile's C=O, making it more electron-poor, so the reaction runs at lower temperature (same product). ② A macrocyclic ring with a cyclohexene inside can come from an intramolecular DA of a long-chain triene — and very high heat runs it backwards (retro-DA spits the diene + dienophile back out).

Mechanisms — one of these IS the 20-pt question

Past mechanism questions: Claisen (2025 A & B), aldol condensation (2025 C), ester from acid chloride (2023). Keys grade arrow start/end points. Practice on blank paper daily Jun 23–25.
Straight from the prof's 2023 lectures: expect ~32 fill-ins + 1–2 mechanisms + 1 retrosynthesis (+ bonus). The mechanism pool he stated out loud: acid chloride + nucleophile (Ch 18) and aldol or Claisen (Ch 19). He will NOT ask Robinson or Michael as a drawn mechanism ("too complex") — those show up as fill-ins and the retrosynthesis instead. Mechanism golden rules: never SN2 at a carbonyl (always two steps through the tetrahedral intermediate); under acid never generate a base, under base never generate an acid; negative Nu = 2 steps, neutral Nu = 3 (extra proton transfer); pyridine just scavenges HCl, never in the product.
🔑 Dr. Hickey's master rule (from his email) — learn this and every acyl mechanism falls out of it.
Every acyl-substitution mechanism you need is built from the same 5 potential steps, in order:

PT → NA → PT → LLG → PT

CodeStepWhat the arrows do
PTProton transferadd or remove a proton (protonate the C=O to activate it, or deprotonate at the end)
NANucleophilic attacknucleophile → carbonyl carbon; C=O π → O. Forms the tetrahedral intermediate
PTProton transfershuffle a proton to turn the leaving group into a good one
LLGLoss of leaving groupO lone pair reforms C=O; C–LG bond breaks. LG = Cl⁻ (acid chloride), ⁻OR (ester), ⁻OCOR (anhydride)
PTProton transferdeprotonate to give the neutral product

How many of the 5 steps? — it depends ONLY on the nucleophile

Nucleophile / conditionsSteps used# stepsExample
Acid-catalyzed attackPT–NA–PT–LLG–PT (all 5)5Fischer esterification; acid hydrolysis of an ester
Neutral nucleophile (H₂O, R′OH, RNH₂)NA–PT–LLG–PT → steps 2, 3, 4, 5*3–4amide from an amine; neutral ester hydrolysis
Negative (anionic) nucleophile (⁻OH, ⁻OR, H⁻)NA–LLG → steps 2 and 4 only2saponification; hydride/Grignard on the acyl carbon
*The prof states neutral attack uses "steps 2, 4, and 5." That's the clean count when the leaving group doesn't need a separate protonation; if it does (e.g. a neutral ⁻OR leaving), you'll also use the middle PT — so neutral = 3 steps minimum. Negative nucleophile = exactly steps 2 and 4 (NA then LLG), because an anion needs no protons added and the product is taken to workup. Acid-catalyzed = all 5.
Two hard rules behind the table: under acid you never make a free base (no ⁻OH/⁻OR floating around) → you protonate first; under base you never make a free acid (no H⁺) → you skip the protonation steps. And never do a one-step SN2 at a carbonyl carbon — always go through the tetrahedral intermediate (NA then LLG are two separate steps).

Master template: nucleophilic acyl substitution (the 5 steps, drawn)

1

2
NA — Nucleophile attacks carbonyl C → tetrahedral intermediate (arrow Nu → C, arrow C=O π → O)
3

Basic conditions: skip 1 & 3 → only 2-4-5 (3 steps). Acid chloride + alcohol: skip ALL proton transfers except final deprotonation (reagent not acidic, Cl⁻ great LG, Nu deprotonated last) → 3 steps. N nucleophiles attack first regardless of conditions. Never SN2 at a carbonyl carbon.

② Aldol addition (NaOH) — 2025 C part 1

1
HO⁻ removes α-H → enolate
Step 1: HO⁻ lone pair → α-H · C–H bond → into C–C(=O) · C=O π → O. Gives the aldehyde enolate.
2
Enolate α-C attacks carbonyl C of the second aldehyde → alkoxide
Step 2: enolate α-C → carbonyl C of aldehyde #2 · C=O π → O. New C–C bond; gives the alkoxide.
3
Alkoxide grabs H from H₂O → β-hydroxy aldehyde (+ regenerated HO⁻)
Step 3: alkoxide O⁻ lone pair → H of H–OH · O–H bond → onto water's O. Gives the β-hydroxy aldehyde (the "aldol").

③ Aldol condensation = addition + E1cb dehydration (NaOH, heat)

4
HO⁻ removes another α-H from the aldol product → enolate
Step 4: HO⁻ → α-H of the aldol product · C–H bond → into C–C(=O). Gives the enolate (carbanion shown) — the β-OH is still there.

Enolate pushes out the β-OH⁻ (arrow: C–C π forms, C–OH bond breaks, OH⁻ leaves) → conjugated enone. This is E1cb, NOT E2 — two separate steps.
Step 5 (E1cb): carbanion lone pair → into the α–β C–C bond (new π) · C–OH bond → onto the leaving OH⁻. Gives the conjugated enone (here: crotonaldehyde).

④ Esterification via acid chloride (CH₃OH, pyridine) — 2023

1
CH₃OH oxygen attacks carbonyl C of RCOCl → tetrahedral intermediate (O still has its H, now positive)
Step 1: arrow from CH₃OH oxygen lone pair → carbonyl C · arrow from C=O π → O. Gives the tetrahedral intermediate (positively charged O — it kept its H).
2
Reform C=O, expel Cl⁻
Step 2: arrow from O⁻ lone pair → C (reform C=O) · arrow from the C–Cl bond → onto Cl. Cl⁻ leaves; O is still protonated (+).
3
Pyridine removes the O–H → neutral ester. (No protonation steps: reagent not acidic, Cl⁻ excellent LG.) Stepwise, never concerted.
Step 3: arrow from pyridine N lone pair → the O–H proton · arrow from O–H bond → onto O (kills the + charge). Neutral ester. Pyridine never appears in the product.

④b Acid chloride hydrolysis (H₂O, pyridine) — 2023 YELLOW mechanism, 24 pts

Same 3-step skeleton as ④: 1) H₂O oxygen attacks carbonyl C → tetrahedral intermediate (O keeps both H's, positive) 2) reform C=O, expel Cl⁻ 3) base (pyridine) removes one O–H → carboxylic acid. The 2023 exam gave 3 pts per arrow set — every arrow drawn from origin to destination.

⑤ Fischer esterification (RCOOH + R′OH, H⁺) — was on Exam 1, still fair game

All 5 template steps: protonate C=O → R′OH attacks → proton transfer (from R′O–H⁺ to an –OH) → expel H₂O → deprotonate. PADPED: Protonate, Add, Deprotonate, Protonate, Eliminate, Deprotonate.

⑥ Tautomerization (acid & base) — short but quizzable

Base: HO⁻ takes α-H → enolate → O protonated by H₂O → enol. Acid: C=O protonated first → H₂O removes α-H → enol. Keto form almost always favored.
